#+latex: \fancyhead[RE,LO]{Abstract}

#+begin_abstract
Access to large-scale spatial audio systems remains a challenge
despite significant interest in the research and commercial spheres.
The cost of highly-multichannel audio interfaces renders such systems
inaccessible to all but the institutions with the deepest
pockets. Described in this thesis is a modular, scalable system for
distributed spatial audio taking advantage of one of a raft of
low-cost embedded audio platforms to have been released over the past
decade or so. Via IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol, hardware modules
remain synchronised to within around
\qty{200}{\nano\second}. Supporting the system is an audio server and
protocols designed for time-sensitive networked audio, control data
and device discovery.
#+end_abstract

#+latex: \fancyhead[RE,LO]{Introduction}

Spatial audio has an accessibility problem. Large-scale spatial and
immersive audio installations remain costly and exclusive, with high
barriers to entry. This thesis describes an attempt to democratise
access to spatial audio systems by taking a distributed approach to
the computation of sound field synthesis algorithms.

\lettrine{A}{udio transmission} over packet-switched networks has
become the /de facto/ standard for a variety of applications over the
past three decades. Replacing the previous generation of cumbersome,
point-to-point installations, it was digital telephony and
videoconferencing that first embraced audio (and video) over the
internet protocol (IP)
suite\nbsp[cite:@hardmanReliable1995;@hardmanSuccessful1998;
@turlettiINRIA1994] and the broadcast industry that pioneered audio
over ethernet\nbsp[cite:@bakkerIntroduction2014]. Before embarking upon a
discussion of networked audio, however, it is worthwhile to review
some fundamental aspects of digita audio and computer networking.

** Digital Audio Signals

In a digital audio system with sampling rate \(f_{s}\), an audio signal
\(y\) is composed of samples \(y[n]\), each representing the amplitude
of the signal at a given point in time \(t\), where \(t = n/f_{s}\). For
arithmetical convenience, sample amplitudes are typically considered
to be floating point numbers, constrained to the range \(y \in [-1,1]\)
(see cref:fig:audio-signal). It is in this form that samples are
typically handled during the processing stage of a DSP algorithm, but
the true underlying representation is concealed, and matters such as
numerical resolution and precision abstracted away.

Samples undergo format conversion at various stages during processing,
such as from an integer pulse code modulation (PCM) filetype to a
stream of floating point audio samples in a digital audio workstation
(DAW), or from a floating point audio stream in an audio device driver
to an integer stream to be handled by a hardware codec. For the most
part a user, and even a developer of audio software, need not concern
themselves with the rudiments of sample representation and conversion;
as shall be shown, however, under certain circumstances these digital
fundamentals must be dealt with directly.

Commonly-encountered sample formats include 16 and 24 bit integer, and
32 bit floating point. Broadly speaking, more bits afford greater
resolution in terms of sample amplitudes that can be represented, with
ramifications for dynamic range and signal-to-noise ratio, as well as
for storage and throughput. Integer formats offer comparatively poor
resolution at low amplitudes due to the mismatch between the linear
distribution of their values versus the logarithmic nature of sound
intensity. Floating point formats address this by having a logarithmic
distribution of avaliable values; the IEEE standard for single
precision (i.e. 32 bit) floating point numbers\nbsp[cite:@IEEE2019]
dictates that precision is greatest around zero, with around half the
available numbers lying in \([-1, 1]\).[fn:12] For brevity's sake, and
for its pertinence later in the text, consider the 16 bit case, and a
single 16 bit integer audio sample:

#+name: lst:bin-sample
#+caption: A 16 bit audio sample, binary representation
#+begin_src bin
11011100 00000010
#+end_src

Separating the bits into two groups of eight is reflective of the fact
that the eight-bit /byte/ is typically the unit of transmission in
computer systems. Grouping the bits in this way points to their
expression in hexadecimal format, transforming the bytes into more
easily-digestible morsels of two digits:

#+name: lst:hex-sample
#+caption: A 16 bit audio sample, hexadecimal representation
#+begin_src hex
dc 02
#+end_src

A number of this kind may also be represented (in C and C++ code for
example) as =0xdc02= with '=0x=' indicating that the number to follow is
in base sixteen. Sixteen bits grant access to \(2^{16} = 65\,536\)
distinct amplitude values for each sample; what cref:lst:hex-sample
tells us is that, in decimal terms, this sample should take the 56
322^{nd} amplitude value.[fn:13] See cref:fig:audio-signal-detail (and
the sample at \(n = 7\)) for an illustration of this.

*** Storage and Transmission

Cref:lst:bin-sample and cref:lst:hex-sample hint at the property of
/endianness/, which is to say the order in wwhich a number's component
bits and bytes appear\nbsp[cite:@cohenHoly1981]. The above representations
mirror the left-to-right nature of western written language and
numbers, being /big endian/ at the levels of both bit and byte, with the
most significant bit (and most significant byte, both abbreviated /MSB/)
appearing first.

Paraphrasing Cohen\nbsp[cite:@cohenHoly1981], if digital audio signals
could be transmitted in their entirety, i.e. the unit of transmission
was /an audio signal/, then endianness would not matter. Practically
speaking, however, in order to be handled by software or hardware, or
transmitted over a network, audio signals must be broken down into
temporally-distributed blocks, those blocks into samples, samples into
bytes, and bytes into bits; correct endianness must be observed with
respect to different computer architectures, file formats, and
transmission protocols.

The PCM WAV file format, for example, di8ctates that the least
significant byte of each sample is stored first --- little endian ---
but that bit order should be big endian\nbsp[cite:@Multimedia1991]; thus
the audio sampling rate in cref:lst:hex-sample should be stored in a
=.wav= file as =0x02dc=. The ethernet standard for communication over
local area computer networks\nbsp[cite:@ieeeIEEE2018] calls for something
akin to the opposite: octets (bytes) are sent /"top to bottom"/, but
each octet is ttransmitted with its least significant bit first.

** Computer Networking

Computer networking is the discipline concerned with the
interconnection of computing systems to enable the exchange of data
and the sharing of resources. With its genesis in the
1960s\nbsp[cite:@bonaventureComputer2011] computer networking addresses
three fundamental concerns: how information is physically or logically
transmitted between nodes; how that transmission is organised and
managed reliably; and how applications address and locate one another
across potentially vast and heterogenous infrastructure.

The field rests on a layered architectural principle --- most famously
codified in the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) reference model and
its more pragmatic counterpart, the TCP/IP
stack\nbsp[cite:@russellRough2006] --- whereby each layer provides
well-defined services to the layer above while abstracting the
complexity below. This separation of concerns allows physical media
(copper, fibre, radio), data-link protocols (ethernet, wi-fi),
network-layer routing (IP), transport-layer protocols (TCP, UDP),
and application-layer semantics to evolve largely independently.

From an engineering standpoint, networking is fundamentally about
managing contention, latency and failure. The shift from
circuit-switched to packet-switched paradigms was a defining
architectural choice; rather than dedicating a physical path for the
duration of a communication session, packet switching multiplexes
variable-length datagrams across shared infrastructure, trading
determinism for efficiency. This trade-off resurfaces throughout the
field --- in QoS mechanisms, congestion control algorithms, and real
time protocols such as IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) where
bounded, deterministic delivery is a hard requirement.

*** Ethernet

Ethernet, first described in the mid 1970s and named for the
/luminiferous ether/ through which historically electromagnetic
radiation was said to propagate\nbsp[cite:@metcalfeEthernet1976], is the
dominant wired local area network technology, standardised under IEEE
802.3\nbsp[cite:@IEEE2018] and comprises the data-link layer foundation of
practically all modern LAN infrastructure. The defining abstraction of
ethernet is the /frame/, a variable-length packet containing source
and destination MAC addresses, an EtherType field indicating the
payload protocol, the payload itself, and a 32-bit cyclic redundancy
checksum for integrity checking.

Originally, ethernet operated over shared
media\nbsp[cite:@metcalfeEthernet1976], coaxial cable, and nodes would
listen before transmitting and back off randomly upon collision
detection. A later transition to switched, full-duplex ethernet
rendered the collision detection algorithm obsolete.

** Audio Over Computer Networks

The transmission of audio data over computer networks has
been a topic of research interest since the earliest days of computer
networking as it is recognised today, i.e. over packet-switched
networks, whereby data to be transmitted is grouped into packets ---
or "datagrams" --- each consisting of a header and a payload. Voice
transmission over ARPANET was being conducted as early as
1974\nbsp[cite:@schulzrinneVoice1992] and the first standard for vocoded
voice communication over packet-switched networks --- the Network
Voice Protocol (NVP) --- was released in
1977\nbsp[cite:@cohenSpecifications1977].

The NVP standard, with control messages for 'calling' and 'ringing',
was clearly intended for digital telephony, and communication was the
primary focus of networked audio research well into the 1990s. Efforts
on supporting real-time voice communication over wide area networks
(WAN) centred on quality of service (QoS), particularly with regard to
the perennial issues of latency, packet loss, and jitter ---
inconsistencies on the rate of packet
transmission\nbsp[cite:@hardmanSuccessful1998; @hardmanReliable1995]. Work
at this time dealt with streams of compressed audio data, and speech
coding algorithms to overcome the deleterious effects of dropped
packets over unreliable network paths and low-bandwidth connections.

Whereas the priority for digital telephony, and later voice over IP
(VoIP) systems, is intelligibility, for musical purposes fidelity is
of greater concern. The late 1990s, with the increasing availability
of high-speed internet connections, saw the beginning of research into
transmitting uncompressed audio data over the
internet\nbsp[cite:@xuRealTime2000; @chafeSimplified2000]. Work of this
sort was spearheaded by the /SoundWIRE/ project, developed by
researchers at McGill University and Stanford University, and took the
form of a wide variety of experiments with high quality audio
transmission over both WAN and local area networks (LAN). These
experiments included LAN-based real-time musical
performances\nbsp[cite:@chafeSimplified2000], concert streaming over
WAN\nbsp[cite:@chafeSimplified2000; @xuRealTime2000], and sonification of
QoS via a distributed digital waveguide dubbed the /Network
Harp/\nbsp[cite:@chafePhysical2002; @chafeSimplified2000].

** Protocols and Systems

VoIP research in the 1990s focused on audio codecs and data
compression\nbsp[cite:@hardmanSuccessful1998; @turlettiINRIA1994], seeking
a compromise with the /best-effort/ nature of internet service. The
SoundWIRE project, in search of high audio quality, turned its
attention directly to the basic transport layer protocols of the
Internet Protocol suite: Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and User
Datagram Protocol (UDP). Chafe et al. characterised their
compression-free system as taking a "simplified approach" to networked
audio\nbsp[cite:@chafeSimplified2000], emphasising the importance of
delivering multichannel audio of at least CD quality (16-bit,
\qty{44.1}{\kHz}) with as little latency as possible.

SoundWIRE experiments included TCP-based concert streaming. TCP's
/connection-oriented/, one-to-one design enables packet flow control
mechanisms that guarantee packet ordering and protect against packet
loss\nbsp[cite:@al-dhiefPerformance2018; @schiavoniAlternatives2013]; at
the expense of increased latency, these mechanisms safeguard quality
of service, and thus audio fidelity; ideal for a remote concert
scenario. UDP by comparison provides no such safeguards, but equally
none of the associated computational or temporal overhead. Further,
due to its /connectionless/ model, /many-to-many/ (multicast) and
/one-to-many/ (broadcast) modes of transmission are possible via address
spaces reserved as part of the internet protocol
standard\nbsp[cite:@meyerIANA2010]. Via UDP, SoundWIRE was able to run as
a distributed digital waveguide over a WAN spanning around
\qty{4500}{\km}\nbsp[cite:@chafeSimplified2000].

From the SoundWIRE project emerged
/JackTrip/\nbsp[cite:@caceresJackTrip2010; @caceresJackTrip2010a], a hybrid
system that couples a TCP handshake with audio transmission over UDP,
thus sidestepping the overhead of TCP packet flow control. Rather than
relying on TCP's built-in mechanisms for stream integrity, JackTrip
supplements UDP with a selection of optional buffering strategies that
aim to optimise its operation in various network conditions. In this
sense it is more flexible than TCP, but in effect JackTrip moulds UDP
transmission into something akin to the connection-oriented model of
TCP, and, in its 'hub server' mode, into a kind of /multiple one-to-one/
design --- multicast transmission is not possible.

UDP has emerged as the protocol of choice for platforms enabling
remote musical collaboration, serving as the basis for
NetJACK\nbsp[cite:@carotNetjack2009], part of the JACK Audio Connection
Kit (a cross-platform audio host), the audiovisual performance
streaming platform LOLA\nbsp[cite:@drioliNetworked2013],
Jamulus\nbsp[cite:@fischerCase2015a],
Soundjack\nbsp[cite:@renaudNetworked2007], and other jamming-focused
platforms, plus more recent entrants, the closed-source but ultimately
UDP-based networking component of Elk Audio OS\nbsp[cite:@turchetElk2021],
for instance. UDP also plays a fundamental role in networked media
streaming, being the typical transport-layer protocol behind the
Real-time Transport Protocol (RTP), and it features in proprietary
networked audio systems such as Dante (Digital Audio Network Through Ethernet)\nbsp[cite:@danteWhat2022].

** AoE in the Audio Industry

In parallel with work being carried out in academia on SoundWIRE,
JackTrip and NetJACK, audio industry bodies --- the IEEE (Institute of
Electrical and Electronics Engineers) and AES (Audio Engineering
Society) standards groups, and companies like Audinate, the creators
of Dante --- were taking an interest in networked audio. Traditional
large-scale audio systems such as those used in broadcast, concert
venues and recording studios rely on the installation of unwieldy
combinations of analogue hardware and cabling, with many potential
points of failure. Seeking literally to lighten the load posed by
"hundreds of kilograms"\nbsp[cite:@bakkerIntroduction2014] of cabling in
analogue audio installations, in the 2000s audio companies were
looking to high speed ethernet as a means to simplify the provision of
high-quality, multichannel audio in industry settings.

Key to these efforts was the release, in 2002, of the IEEE 1588
standard for the Precision Time Protocol, a means by which networked
computer systems can achieve clock
synchronicity\nbsp[cite:@edisonIEEE15882002]. PTP (another protocol that
typically uses UDP for transport) superseded the lower-resolution
Network Time Protocol (NTP)\nbsp[cite:@millsImproved1994], and, under
ideal conditionns, can achieve sychronisation accuracy of
sub-microsecond order\nbsp[cite:@tongzhouResearch2022]. Synchronisation is
achieved via the exchange of timestamped packets, coupled with precise
estimates for send and receive times. Precision is best when
timestamps can be calculated at the /physical layer/ --- layer 1 of the
OSI model, of which the aforementioned transport layer (layer 3) is a
component --- i.e. by dedicated timers at the level of the physical
network interface. Legacy and low-cost networking equipment do not
typically possess support for hardware timestamping,
however\nbsp[cite:@correllDesign2005], and devices that do offer such
support are often markedly more expensive, though notable exceptions
do exist. PTP can be deployed as a software-only
implementation\nbsp[cite:@correllDesign2005], albeit with impaired
accuracy and a protracted clock-convergence period.

Dante, with its promise of low-latency, highly-multichannel audio over
wired LAN, and device synchronisation via hardware PTP, has become the
/de facto/ industry standard in networked
audio\nbsp[cite:@bakkerIntroduction2014]. Bakker et al. refer to Dante as
an "open" system, which is true, perhaps, in the sense that companies
can incorporate the Dante system into their products under licence
from Audinate; from the perspective of the academic community,
however, Dante is very much a closed-source initiative and not a
suitable platform for research.

In 2011, IEEE released the Audio Video Bridging (AVB, IEEE 802.1)
standard\nbsp[cite:@ieeeIEEE2011], and AES67 followed in
2013\nbsp[cite:@hildebrandAES6720132014]. These technical standards
describe /suites/ of protocols for tasks such as media transmission,
device discovery and synchronisation, ind interoperability with other
systems. Both use PTP fo device synchronisation; AES67 uses RTP for
media transmission whereas AVB uses the data-link layer (layer 2)
Audio Video Transport Protocol. Open implementations of AVB and AES67
exist, but, being complex standards featuring many components, such
implementations may not be complete, and support for embedded
platforms is limited.[fn:9]

** Audio Spatialisation

Audio spatialisation is, plainly put, the practice of distributing
sound in space. The spatialisation of /primary sound sources/, e.g.,
sound captured by microphones, stored as digital audio files, or
synthesised in real-time, can be achieved simply by delivering those
primary sources to /secondary sound sources/, i.e., loudspeakers or
headphones. Exploiting auditory cues, and the nature of the
propagation of sound, it is possible to suggesnt the presence of
primary sources at arbitrary locations, independent of the secondary
source distribution. The motivation behind audio spatialisation, then,
is to create (or indeed /recreate/) sonic environments for creative and
immersive purposes, such as for virtual reality experiences, in
cinematic settings, for music production or art installations, to give
but a handful of examples.

A number of techniques exisnt for what is termed /sound field
synthesis/\nbsp[cite:@nicolSound2017; @ahrensAnalytic2012], all of which
essentially take the form of applying some manner of /driving function/
to an input audio signal to generate an appropriate driving signal to
be delivered to a secondary sound source in the listening
environment\nbsp[cite:@ahrensAnalytic2012]. For a loudspeaker at position
\(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}x & y & z\end{bmatrix}^{T}\), the
time-domain driving signal \(\hat{d}(\mathbf{x}, t)\) can be expressed
as a convolution of the input signal \(\hat{s}_{\text{in}}(t)\) and the
driving function \(d(\mathbf{x},t)\):

#+NAME: eq:driving-signal
\begin{equation}
\hat{d}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \hat{s}_{\text{in}}(t) \ast d(\mathbf{x}, t)\;,
\end{equation}
where \(t\) denotes time.

Commonly-employed approaches to sound fdield creation can be grouped
into two broad categories: amplitude- and time-based panning
techniques, and physical sound field recreation approaches.

*** Periphony and Binaural Reproduction

The former, /periphonic/, types encompass stereophony and surround-sound
systems, consisting of secondary sources in a planar arrangement
equidistant from the listening position. The techniques exploit the
interaural level difference (ILD) cue, i.e., the difference in
perceived amplitude relative to the listener's
ears\nbsp[cite:@ziemerWave2020; @verheijenSound1998; @pulkkiVirtual1997],
te encourage the listener to localise sound to a position on the
circumference of an arc or circle around the listening position. For
systems of this sort, the driving function is a constant scalar value,
or, for a moving phantom source, a time-varying function that returns
a scalar value. Such periphonic approaches can extend to three
dimensions in the case of vector base amplitude panning
(VBAP)\nbsp[cite:@pulkkiVirtual1997], which uses trios of speakers to
position phantom sources on the surface of a sphere with the listening
position at its origin.

Time-based panning effects, by contrast, make use of the interaural
time difference (ITD) cue to give the impression of a phantom sounce
located toward the loudspeaker producing the singal at the earliest
time\nbsp[cite:@verheijenSound1998; @pulkkiVirtual1997]. Thus the driving
function for a time-based panning system is a delay of the form

#+NAME: eq:time-based-driving-function
\begin{equation}
d(\mathbf{x}, t) = \delta(t - \tau)\;,
\end{equation}
where \(\tau\) is the duration of the delay.

The effects of ILD and ITD cues transfer to headphone-based listening,
in which case, rather than periphonic, they form a sort of /in-head/
localisation\nbsp[cite:@ahrensAnalytic2012]. For a significantly more
naturalistic auditory outcome, ILD and ITD cues, when combined with
filters describing the dispersive and absorptive effects of the head,
torso and outer ears, constitute a Head-Related Transfer Function
(HRTF), the key component in what is termed /binaural
reproduction/. Binaural recordings are taken either with a dummy head
or ear-mounted microphones, and thus the signal for each ear is
coloured by the head used during recording, or HRTF measurements can
be taken and used to describe filters to be applied to arbitrary
signals at playback. Binaural sound is suited to headphone-based
listening but ma,y be achieved with loudspeakers if suitable
cross-talk cancellation is applied\nbsp[cite:@kaiserTransaural2011].

Periphonic approaches are subject to the phenomenon of an ideal
listening position, or /sweet-spot/\nbsp[cite:@nicolSound2017], that is a
listening position away from which the spatialisation effect is
significantly degraded. Binaural reproduction, if not coupled with
head motion tracking, is similarly afflicted by an ideal position and
orientation\nbsp[cite:@verheijenSound1998]; further, for faithful
reproduction, HRTFs should be
individualised\nbsp[cite:@depoliPhysically1998]. As such, these techniques
are not suited to collective and immersive auditory experiences
whereby multiple participants may move freely about their environment.

*** Physically-inspired Techniques

Physical approaches fall into two main types: wave field synthesis
(WFS)\nbsp[cite:@berkhoutAcoustic1993] and ambisonics (and higher-order
ambisonics --- HOA)\nbsp[cite:@zotterAmbisonics2019]. Rather than directly
manipulating sound localisation cues, these types seek to trigger
those cues indirectly by synthesising a sound field as if it had been
created by "true" acoustic sources.

In the case of ambisonics, first described in the early 1970s by
Michael Gerzon\nbsp[cite:@ahrensAnalytic2012], the sound field is
decomposed into /spherical harmonics/, spatial functions described by
linear sums of directional components of increasing
order\nbsp[cite:@nicolSound2017]. Like periphonic approaches, ambisonics
suffers from a sweet-spot effect which worsens with attempts to
reproduce sounds of higher frequency, but can be mitigated by
reproducing higher-order modes and increasing the density of the
distribution of secondary sources.

WFS is based upon Huygens' principle, originating in the field of
optics, which states that a propagating wavefront can be recreated by
a distribution of secondary point
sources\nbsp[cite:@bellochPerformance2021; @berkhoutAcoustic1993;
@muellerAcoustic1971] (see cref:fig:holophony). WFS is variously
termed a form of /acoustic holography/ or
/holophony/\nbsp[cite:@berkhoutHolographic1988;
@ahrensAnalytic2012]. Effectively, by timing the reporudction of an
input signal at an array of secondary sources, a wavefront associated
with a virtual sound source can be synthesised. To simulate auditory
cues related to perceived distance, a filter can be applied to model
losses to the virtual medium of acoustic propagation. The principle
assumes a continuous array of secondary sources but of course in
practice it is necessary to use a discrete array of loudspeakers,
which, much as is the case for HOA, has consequences for spatial
resolution; to mitigate the issue of spatial aliasing, whereby sounds
of higher frequency cannot be recreated
unambiguously\nbsp[cite:@winterGeometric2018], secondary sources should be
placed very close together. Consequently, to serve a large listening
area, many speakers, and thus many audio channels, are required.

Via appropriate timing of the delivery of a primary sound source to
the secondary source array, it is possible to synthesise virtual sound
sources, plane waves, and /focused/ sound sources, corresponding with
concave, flat, and convex synthesised wavefronts respectively; the
latter, dependent on the location of the listener, appear to emanate
from within the real sound field, rather than its virtual counterpart.

Focusing on the former kind, however, for \(m\) virtual sources, the
time-domain driving signal, \(\hat{d}\) for the secondary source at
\(\mathbf{x}\) may be expressed as a sum of input-signal
driving-function convolutions (see cref:eq:driving-signal):

#+NAME: eq:wfs-driving-signal
\begin{equation}
\hat{d}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{s}_{\text{in},k} \ast d_{k}(\mathbf{x}, t)\;,
\end{equation}
where the driving function \(d_{k}\) is\nbsp[cite:@ahrensAnalytic2012]:

#+NAME: eq:wfs-driving-function
\begin{equation}
d_{k}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \frac{y_{k}}{r_{k}}f(t) \ast \delta\left(t - \frac{r_{k}}{c}\right)\;.
\end{equation}
This is, in effect, a physically-informed extension of
cref:eq:time-based-driving-function. The /WFS prefilter/, \(f(t)\), is a
function that simulates the absorption of energy into the simulated
medium of acoustic propagation. The delta function \(\delta\) has the
effect of delaying the prefilter, and thus \(s_{\text{in},k}\), by the
time of propagation for a medium of with propagation speed \(c\)
(typically modelled as \qty{343}{\meter\per\second} for sound in
air). The components of the driving function are depicted in
cref:fig:driving-function.

As described, for optimal spatial resolution, systems implementing HOA
and WFS require many output channels; in effect, the more channels,
and the greater the loudspeaker-density, the better.

The Multisensory Experience Lab at Aalborg University (AAU),
Copenhagen, hosts a 64-channel, square-array WFS system covering an

desktop computer, connected, via a USB MADI (Multichannel Audio
Digital Interface) interface, to two 32-channel MADI to analogue
converters. Though this system's WFS engine is provided by open-source
/WFSCollider/ software,[fn:3] being a centralised system, the computer
that co-ordinates its operation is powerful --- 12-core CPU, 64 GB RAM
--- and was costly at the time of purchase; likely on the order of
several thousand Euros. With further regard to cost, the current
equivalent MADI to analogue converter models are priced at roughly
€5,000 apiece. Excluding the cost of loudspeakers (and the gantry upon
which they are mounted) one may reasonably place an estimate of €250
per output channel on this system.

The world's largest dedicated WFS system, at TU Berlin, features over
800 output channels served by a distributed cluster of fifteen
computers acting as audio nodes and two additional control
computers\nbsp[cite:@baalmanRenewed2007].[fn:8] The fifteen audio nodes of
the TU Berlin system are each equipped with a MADI audio interface,
connected to a MADI to ADAT bridge; these can, at the time of writing,
be purchased for around €1,400 and €4,500 each, respectively, for a
total of €88,500. Imagining €2,000 per computer, a conservative
estimate of €150/channel may be reached, however the expense
associated with this system is difficult to assess as it is tied to
the concert hall space in which it resides. indeed, one thing besides
expense that unites the systems at AAU and TU Berlin, plus the
345-speaker hybrid WFS/HOA system at IRCAM
(Paris)\nbsp[cite:@noisternigDispositif2012], the 512-channel WFS system
at the Rensslaer Polytecnic Institute (NY, United States)[fn:10], and
the behemoth installation at /The Sphere/ (Las Vegas, United
States)[fn:11] is their /in-situ/ nature; these are site-specific
systems with, at best, limited flexibility.

What one is afforded by WFS installations of this scale and expense,
however, is high quality audio reproduction with tantamount to perfect
output synchronicity; the integrity of the holophonic effect of WFS
is, unavoidable matters of spatial aliasing aside, guaranteed.

** Distributed Audio Systems

As alluded to earlier in this chapter, our aim is to distribute the
problem of audio spatialisation, and it is worthwhile to revisit why
this is the case. In the broadest terms, a distributed system is /"a
collection of independent entities that cooperate to solve a problem
that cannot be individually
solved"/\nbsp[cite:@kshemkalyaniDistributed2011]. An ideal distributed
system is characterised by: /modularity/, being comprised of separate,
interchangeable entities; /scalability/, being extensible without
incurring a performance penalty to the system as a whole, and;
/improved performance/cost ratio/, since it can be constructed to meet
the proportion that circumstances require, with the minimum degree of
redundancy. Additionally, for algorithms that can be effectively
/parallelised/, a distributed system may provide more aggregate
computational power than its centralised counterpart.

Where a distributed system may suffer, by contrast, is in terms of
reliability. Nodes in a distributed computational system must be
served with power and access to the data they require in order to
operate, which entails a proliferation of potential points of
failure. The other side to the coin of modularity is a concern
regarding the programmability of such a system; ensuring that all
entities possess up-to-date instructions for operation may not be
trivial. Further, some algorithms may be better suited to
parallelisation than others; efficient use of increased computational
resources is not guaranteed.

Distributed audio processing is by no means a matter without
precedent. (Indeed, the WFS installation at TU Berlin described in
cref:sec:sota-spatial-audio is of course a distributed system, albeit
not an especially accessible one.) A selection of prior work in
distributed DSP and audio spatialisation, plus systems incorporating
microcontrollers and single-board computers is detailed below.

*** State of the Art Distributed Audio Systems

Applications of SoundWIRE to what its creators termed /Internet
Acoustics/\nbsp[cite:@chafePhysical2002] clearly stand as examples of

reverberator\nbsp[cite:@chafeAm2018], or /"transcontinental echo chamber"/
[cite:@chafeSimplified2000], plus the aforementioned /Network
Harp/. Experiments of this sort were intended initially as
sonifications of QoS --- a characteristic of network systems that is
difficult to represent in real time in graphical or textual form due
to the ephemeral nature of the phenomena of jitter and packet loss ---
but stand as fascinating applications in their own right of digital

has focused on optimising networked audio less as a creative tool in

#+latex: \minitoc
#+latex: \newpage

Differentiable programming via automatic differentiation (AD) is the
foundation for gradient-based optimisation techniques, and forms the
basis for many current approaches to machine learning. Though well
catered-for in general-purpose programming languages, the availability
of AD in a domain-specific language (DSL) could offer novel
perspectives on optimisation problems in the field of audio. In this
chapter a general scheme is presented for differentiable programming
in the Faust programming language, a high-performance DSL tailored to
audio synthesis and signal processing. Faust's rich ecosystem, coupled
with a comprehensive AD implementation, can provide support for audio
optimisation and machine learning applications on a multitude of
platforms, from FPGAs to the web.

** Introduction

Gradient-based methods are ubiquitous in tackling a wide variety of
mathematical optimisation and machine learning problems, and the
availability of such methods hinges on the possibility of computing
the derivatives of computer programs. In the field of machine learning
in particular, automatic differentiation
(AD)\nbsp[cite:@baydinAutomatic2018] has emerged as the dominant
differentiable programming paradigm. In AD, the primitive operations
of a programming language are defined differentiably, thus programs
composed from those operations are in turn differentiable end-to-end
with respect to their input variables. In a gradient-based
optimisation scenario, the partial derivatives of an algorithm
terminating in a loss function constitute a gradient vector, which can
be backpropagated, causing the algorithm's input variables, weights in
a neural network perhaps, to approach optimal values. Techniques of
this sort employed in the audio domain are referred to as
/differentiable digital signal processing/
(DDSP)\nbsp[cite:@hayesReview2024], and find applications from least mean
squares (LMS) filtering\nbsp[cite:@alexandreExperimental2023] to
differentiable audio synthesis\nbsp[cite:@engelDifferentiable2020].

Building on previous work on AD in the Faust programming
language\nbsp[cite:@rushtonDifferentiable2024], here Faust's pattern
matching abstraction is exploited to extend to a more general AD
syntax, demonstrating that arbitrary compositions of signal processing
blocks can be differentiated, and to higher order if desired. With AD
in Faust, it is possible to create differentiable infinite impulse
response (IIR) filters and neural network building blocks. This work
provides the basis for an audio-focused ecosystem for the development
of solutions to problems based on machine learning.

Faust is a functional paradigm, domain specific language for audio
digital signal processing\nbsp[cite:@orlareyFAUST2009]. It is built atop a
domain-agnostic /block diagram
algebra/\nbsp[cite:@orlareyAlgebra2002;@orlareySyntactical2004] that
describes primitive operations connected into graphs via a selection
of /composition operators/ (see cref:fig:compositions). A textual
language, Faust can, for example, define a diagram consisting of two
blocks, \(A\) and \(B\), composed in sequence, with code of the form
#+name: lst:seq
#+caption: Sequential composition of two Faust blocks.
#+begin_src faust :noeval
process = A : B;
#+end_src

Conceptually, a given block diagram, \(D\), may undergo semantic
transformation, notated \(\llbracket \cdot \rrbracket\), into a signal
processor\nbsp[cite:@orlareySyntactical2004]:[fn:7]
#+NAME: eq:semanticTransformation
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \llbracket D \rrbracket &\;:\; \mathbb{S}^{n} \to \mathbb{S}^{m} \\
    \llbracket D \rrbracket(s_{1},\dots,s_{n}) &\to (y_{1},\dots,y_{m}) \\
    \mathbf{y}(t) &= f(\mathbf{s}(t))\;,
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbb{S}\) is the set of all signals, \(f\) is a
mathematical function analogous to the primitive operations and
compositions of \(D\), and \(t\) is time. The signal processor
receives a vector of input signals, \(\mathbf{s}\), and produces a
vector of output signals, \(\mathbf{y}\).

*** The Faust Ecosystem

Faust is a highly optimised, compiled language\nbsp[cite:@orlareyNew2020],
with C++ as its default compilation target. One of the great strengths
of the Faust ecosystem is its /write once, run anywhere/ model; a
variety of additional backends are supported, including
LLVM\nbsp[cite:@letzOverview2018], WebAssembly\nbsp[cite:@letzCompiling2017],
and Rust\nbsp[cite:@gasterAudio2020], plus numerous software and hardware
platforms. Faust code can be compiled for use in VST
plugins\nbsp[cite:@albouyFaust2017], for embedded
systems\nbsp[cite:@michonEmbedded2020] and
\mbox{FPGAs}\nbsp[cite:@cochardProgramming2024]; it is used by artists and
digital
luthiers\nbsp[cite:@michonFaust2020a;@carenKeyWI2020;@hansenWeather2018],
and features sophisticated libraries for wave digital filter
modelling\nbsp[cite:@roosenburgWave2021] and physical modelling
synthesis\nbsp[cite:@russoIntroducing2021;@michonFaust2018], amongst
others.

*** Differentiable Programming in Faust
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:diffprog
:END:

In light of Faust's versatility, efficiency, and strengths as a DSL in
tackling audio problems it may come as little surprise that it has
been turned to machine learning problems in the audio domain, via
/DawDreamer/\nbsp[cite:@braunDawDreamer2021], which uses a Python backend
to transpile Faust code for gradient-based optimisation with the JAX
Python library. JAX, and other well-known machine learning frameworks,
such as TensorFlow and PyTorch, enable gradient-based optimisation
approaches by way of /automatic differentiation/, a technique for
computing the numerical outputs of both a mathematical expression (or,
more commonly, a computer program) and its derivatives in
parallel\nbsp[cite:@rallArithmetic1986;@baydinAutomatic2018;@harrisonBrief2021].

Differentiable programming\nbsp[cite:@wangDemystifying2019] is, broadly
speaking, the creation of computer programs using primitive operations
which are themselves differentiable; a computer program consisting of
differentiable primitives may be differentiated automatically,
producing the desired /primal/ and /gradient/ numerical outputs for
use in a gradient-based optimisation algorithm. In this vein, a
Faust-based LMS algorithm has been used for real-time acoustic
control\nbsp[cite:@alexandreExperimental2023], though the DDSP aspect of
this work was limited to the particular application. The feasibility
of a more general approach to DDSP in the Faust language has been
demonstrated via the implementation of forward-mode AD
primitives\nbsp[cite:@rushtonDifferentiable2024].

One way of framing forward-mode AD is as an arithmetic of dual
numbers\nbsp[cite:@rallArithmetic1986;@yuDNAD2013] of the form \(\langle{}u,\nabla{}u\rangle\),
where \(u\) is the aforementioned primal, and \(\nabla{}u
= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial{}u}{\partial{}x_{1}} & \cdots & \frac{\partial{}u}{\partial{}x_{N}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\),
the gradient of \(u\), its partial derivatives with respect to a
vector of \(N\) input variables, \(\mathbf{x}\). A dual number can be
passed into a forward-mode differentiable primitive, \(f\), yielding
an output dual number, \(\langle{}f(u),\nabla{}uf'(u)\rangle\). Faust is a language of
/signals/ and signal processors; consequently we will refer to the
/dual signal/, \(\langle{}s,\nabla{}s\rangle \in \mathbb{S}_{d}\), where \(\mathbb{S}_{d} =
\mathbb{S}^{N+1}\). The signal processor based on the differentiated
block diagram \(D\) has the following semantic scheme:
#+NAME: eq:diffSemantic
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket &\;:\; \mathbb{S}_{d}^{M} \to \mathbb{S}_{d}^{P} \\
\Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket(\langle{}s_{1},\nabla{}s_{1}\rangle,&\dots,\langle{}s_{M},\nabla{}s_{M}\rangle) \\
&\to (\langle{}y_{1},\nabla{}y_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle{}y_{P},\nabla{}y_{P}\rangle) \\
\langle\mathbf{y}(t), \;\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(t)\rangle &= \langle{}f(\mathbf{s}(t)), \;\mathbf{J}_{f}(t)\mathbf{J}_{{\mathbf{s}}}(t)\rangle\;,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta_{N}\llbracket \cdot \rrbracket\) indicates derivative
transformation with respect to \(N\) input variables, and
\(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}\), \(\mathbf{J}_{f} = \nabla{}f(\mathbf{s})\), and
\(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{s}} = \nabla\mathbf{s}\) are, respectively, \(P\times{}N\),
\(P\times{}M\) and \(M\times{}N\) Jacobian matrices.

AD primitives have been demonstrated in Faust using the language's
pattern matching
abstraction\nbsp[cite:@rushtonDifferentiable2024]. Pattern matching is a
source code transformation mechanism, and exploits the fact that a
Faust function may receive, as a parameter, a programmatic
expression. For example, a differentiable addition primitive, acting
on dual signals \(\langle{}s_{1}, \nabla{}s_{1}\rangle,\langle{}s_{2}, \nabla{}s_{2}\rangle\), may be created by defining a
function that accepts the addition operator as a parameter, and
produces, in parallel, the addition primitive's primal output,
\(s_{1}+s_{2}\), and its gradients, \(\nabla{}s_{1}+\nabla{}s_{2}\).
#+name: lst:diffAdd
#+caption: Definition of a differentiable addition primitive in the
#+caption: Faust language.
#+attr_latex: :float t
#+begin_src faust :noeval
env(vars) = environment {
  // ...
  diff(+) = interleave(vars.N + 1, 2)
    : +,par(i, vars.N, +);
  // ...
};
#+end_src
A dual-signal addition operator implementation is given in
cref:lst:diffAdd; the operator is defined within a /differentiable
environment/, which receives and enumerates a vector of input
variables, ensuring that all derivative transformations produce the
appropriate number of partial derivatives; the function ~interleave~
delivers the primal signals and their respective partial derivatives
to the addition operations in the appropriate order: \(s_{1}, s_{2},
\frac{\partial{}s_{1}}{\partial{}x_{1}}, \frac{\partial{}s_{2}}{\partial{}x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial{}s_{1}}{\partial{}x_{N}}, \frac{\partial{}s_{2}}{\partial{}x_{N}}\),
as illustrated in cref:fig:diffadd. One addition primitive is created
for the primal operation, and a further \(N\) additions in parallel,
which we can notate \(\big\|_{l=1}^{N} +\), for computing partial
derivatives.

*** Motivation

Considering the plethora of existing approaches to
DDSP\nbsp[cite:@hayesReview2024], why pursue an implementation in Faust?
The availability of the Faust-to-JAX transpiler would appear to
obviate such an initiative, but for the fact that the AD
transformations facilitated by JAX lie the wrong side of the
transpilation step. A comprehensive differentiable programming
framework in the Faust language itself would allow DDSP algorithms to
be written once, and run anywhere. A domain-specific approach to
audio-oriented machine learning is not without precedent; indeed, a
suite of ML-centric tools, with an emphasis on human computer
interaction, exists for the ChucK programming
language\nbsp[cite:@liChAI2024]. Faust, however, with its functional
approach, and cross-platform capabilities, offers a unique point of
vantage.

The observation has been made that the relative structures of neural
networks and digital filters bear striking
similarities\nbsp[cite:@kuznetsovDifferentiable2020]. There is a very real
sense in which machine learning problems, and gradient-based
optimisation problems in general, are signal processing problems. The
possibility of turning a signal processing DSL to problems of this
sort paves the way to novel approaches and fresh perspectives on this
dynamic field of research.

In Faust's block diagram algebra, a given block diagram, \(D\), is
comprised of a primitive block, \(P\), or the composition of two block
diagrams via a composition operator according to the syntactic
rule
#+NAME: eq:syntax
\begin{equation}
D = P \;|\; (D_{1} \diamond D_{2})\;,
\end{equation}
where \(\diamond\) is one of the sequential (:), parallel (,), split (<:),
merge (:>), or recursive (\(\sim\)) composition operators. The creation
of differentiable Faust programs has been shown to be possible by
/undifferentiated/ composition of differentiated primitive
blocks\nbsp[cite:@rushtonDifferentiable2024], the syntactic rule being
#+NAME: eq:badDiffSyntax
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket = \Delta_{N}\llbracket P_{1} \rrbracket \diamond \Delta_{N}\llbracket P_{2} \rrbracket \diamond \dots \diamond \Delta_{N}\llbracket P_{n} \rrbracket\;.
\end{equation}
While effective in facilitating differentiable programming, this
syntax imposed an undesirable verbosity. Our preferred syntactic rule
should be a direct transformation of cref:eq:syntax:
#+NAME: eq:idealDiffSyntax
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket
  = \Delta_{N}\llbracket P \rrbracket \;|\; \Delta_{N}\llbracket D_{1} \diamond D_{2} \rrbracket \;.
\end{equation}
The ideal outcome is to permit the programmer to subject an entire
Faust program to automatic differentiation in a single expression,
minimising the amount of re-writing required to ready their code for
transformation. This would render a great proportion of existing Faust
code differentiable, and, in doing so, prepare that wealth of material
for prospective use in optimisation problems, and machine learning
applications. In order to achieve such an outcome, it is necessary to
provide differentiation rules for the five composition operators,
taking into account their arithmetical significance, if any, in a
block diagram to which they contribute.

*** Differentiable Block-Diagram Compositions

To define syntactic rules for the derivatives of Faust's composition
operators entails analysing them as if they were themselves primitive
blocks. To do this, we must consider them in tandem with rules for
some of Faust's fundamental primitive operators; these will assist us
in achieving differentiable compositions.

**** Identity Primitive

Faust's identity function, notated \(\_\), describes a connection
between two blocks. Treated as a signal processor, it adheres to the
following semantic scheme\nbsp[cite:@orlareySyntactical2004]:
#+NAME: eq:identity
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \llbracket \_ \rrbracket &\;:\; \mathbb{S} \to \mathbb{S} \\
    \llbracket \_ \rrbracket(s) &\to (s)\;.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
It receives one signal and returns that same signal unchanged,
analogous to multiplying it by a constant signal of value
\(1\). In dual signal arithmetic, this is
#+NAME: eq:dualIdentity
\begin{align}
\langle{}s,\nabla{}s\rangle\langle{}1,\nabla1\rangle
  = \langle{}1s, 1\nabla{}s + s\nabla{}1 \rangle
  = \langle{}s,\nabla{}s\rangle\;,
\end{align}
and the semantic rule for the derivative of the identity function is
thus
#+NAME: eq:diffidentity
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket &\;:\; \mathbb{S}_{d} \to \mathbb{S}_{d} \\
    \Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket(\langle{}s, \nabla{}s\rangle) &\to (\langle{}s, \nabla{}s\rangle)\;.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
The dual signal \(\langle{}s,\nabla{}s\rangle\) being composed of one
primal signal and \(N\) gradient signals, we can further state that
#+NAME: eq:diffidentity2
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket = \big\|_{l=1}^{N+1}\;\_\;.
\end{equation}

**** Parallel Composition

Blocks composed in parallel (see cref:fig:par) do not influence
one-another, arithmetically-speaking. The outputs of the program
\(A,B\), \(y_{1},\dots,y_{o_{A}}\) and \(y_{o_{A}+1},\dots,y_{o_{A}+o_{B}}\), are
essentially the outputs of separate block diagrams; thus
\(\Delta_{N}\llbracket A,B \rrbracket = \Delta_{N}\llbracket A \rrbracket,\Delta_{N}\llbracket
B \rrbracket\), and, more generally
#+NAME: eq:diffpar
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{N}\left\llbracket\big\|_{l=1}^{L}D_{l}\right\rrbracket =
  \big\|_{l=1}^{L}\Delta_{N}\llbracket D_{l} \rrbracket \;.
\end{equation}

Implicit in cref:eq:semanticTransformation,eq:diffSemantic are the
input and output signals of the Faust block diagram, \(D\), and the
following equivalence applies:
\begin{align}
D &= \big\|_{l=1}^{i_{D}}\,\_ \;:\; D\label{eq:diffSeqIn} \\
D &= D \;:\; \big\|_{l=1}^{o_{D}}\,\_ \;,
\end{align}
where \(i_{D}\) and \(o_{D}\) are, respectively, the number of input and
output signals of \(D\). Applying derivative transformation to
cref:eq:diffSeqIn, and bringing cref:eq:diffpar,eq:diffidentity2 to
bear,
#+NAME: eq:diffImplicitIn
\begin{align}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket &= \Delta_{N}\llbracket \big\|_{l=1}^{i_{D}}\,\_ \;:\; D \rrbracket \nonumber \\
  &= \big\|_{l=1}^{i_{D}}\big\|_{m=1}^{N+1}\,\_ \;:\; \Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket \nonumber \\
  &= \big\|_{l=1}^{i_{D}}\Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket \;:\; \Delta_{N}\llbracket D \rrbracket\;.
\end{align}
The arithmetical transparency of sequential composition holds in the
general case; expanding on the semantic scheme in cref:eq:diffSemantic,
\(\Delta_{N}\llbracket A : B \rrbracket\) is analogous to composing two
functions, \(g\) and \(f\):
#+NAME: eq:diffSeqSemantic
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket A : B \rrbracket(\langle{}\mathbf{s}, &\;\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{s}}\rangle)
  \to (\langle{}\mathbf{y}, \;\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}\rangle) \\[5pt]
\langle{}\mathbf{y}(t), \;\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(t)\rangle
  &= f(g(\langle\mathbf{s}(t), \;\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{s}}(t)\rangle)) \\
  &= \big\langle{}f(g(\mathbf{s}(t))), \;\mathbf{J}_{f}(t)\mathbf{J}_{g}(t)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{s}}(t)\big\rangle\;.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
As illustrated in cref:fig:dualSec, the composition of functions of dual
signals has the effect of primal function composition on the one hand,
and application of the chain rule of differentiation on the other, and
works so long as \(o_{A} = i_{B}\),[fn:2] thus
#+NAME: eq:diffSeq
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket A : B \rrbracket = \Delta_{N}\llbracket A \rrbracket : \Delta_{N}\llbracket B \rrbracket\;.
\end{equation}

**** Split Composition

Composing \(A\) and \(B\), \(i_{B} = ko_{A}\), with the split
composition operator (see cref:fig:split) duplicates the output
signals of \(A\), \(k\) times, passing each group of duplicates to
\(B\), in order:
#+NAME: eq:split
\begin{equation}
A <: B = A \;:\; \big\|_{l=1}^{o_{A}}\,\_ \;<:\; \big\|_{m=1}^{k}\big\|_{l=1}^{o_{A}}\,\_ \;:\; B\;.
\end{equation}
Signal order is maintained within each group of duplicates, so,
applying derivative transformation to both sides of cref:eq:split, and
recalling cref:eq:diffImplicitIn,
#+NAME: eq:diffsplit
\begin{align}
&\quad\;\Delta_{N}\llbracket A <: B \rrbracket \nonumber\\
  &= \Delta_{N}\llbracket A \rrbracket :
    \big\|_{l=1}^{o_{A}}\Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket <:
    \big\|_{m=1}^{k}\big\|_{l=1}^{o_{A}}\Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket :
    \Delta_{N}\llbracket B \rrbracket \nonumber \\
  &= \Delta_{N}\llbracket A \rrbracket <: \Delta_{N}\llbracket B \rrbracket \;.
\end{align}

**** Cut Primitive
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:cut
:END:

The cut primitive, notated !, terminates a signal, and combinations of
the cut and identity functions can be used to create complex signal
routings.  For instance, to interleave two groups of two signals, such
that \(s_{1,1},s_{1,2},s_{2,1},s_{2,2}\) becomes \(s_{1,1},s_{2,1},s_{1,2},s_{2,2}\), one may
write
#+NAME: eq:interleave22
\begin{equation}
\_,\_,\_,\_ \;<:\; \_,!,\_,!,!,\_,!,\_\;.
\end{equation}
To differentiate an expression of this kind, the appropriate
accompaniment to cref:eq:diffidentity2,eq:diffsplit for the derivative
of the cut primitive is
#+NAME: eq:cut
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \Delta_{N}\llbracket ! \rrbracket &= \big\|_{l=1}^{N+1}!\;.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
A general interleaving function, as used in cref:lst:diffAdd, can be
written using the cut and identity primitives, plus parallel and split
composition (see cref:lst:interleave). Comprising only differentiable
elements, it too is differentiable.
#+name: lst:interleave
#+caption: A Faust function, \(\chi(r,c)\) (see cref:eq:interleave for its
#+caption: mathematical definition), that interleaves \(c\) groups of \(r\)
#+caption: signals into \(r\) groups of \(c\) signals.
#+attr_latex: :options float=ht
#+begin_src faust :noeval
interleave(r, c) = par(l, r * c, _)
  <: par(m, r,
       par(n, c, cutpq(m, r - m - 1)))
with {
  cutpq(0, q) = _,par(l, q, !);
  cutpq(p, 0) = par(l, p, !),_;
  cutpq(p, q) =
    par(l, p, !),cutpq(0, q);
};
#+end_src

**** Delay Primitive
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:delay
:END:

Faust provides two primitives for delaying signals; here we will focus
on the ~mem~ primitive, which, when composed in sequence with a
signal, applies a one-sample delay.  In general, a fixed delay can be
represented as the convolution of a signal, \(s(x,t)\), with a
time-shifted impulse [cite:@mcclellanSignal2003],
#+NAME: eq:delayAsConv
\begin{equation}
s(x, t-\tau) = s(x, t) \ast h(t)\;,\quad h(t) = \delta(t - \tau)\;.
\end{equation}
Taking the temporal-parametric Fourier transform of the derivative of
the convolution with respect to \(x\), then using the convolution and
differentiation properties of the
transform\nbsp[cite:@mcclellanSignal2003], yields
#+NAME: eq:timeinvariantdiff
\begin{align}
&\quad\;\mathcal{F}\left[\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{}x}\left(s(x,t) \ast h(t)\right)\right] \nonumber\\
  &= j\omega_{x}S(j\omega_{x},j\omega_{t})H(j\omega_{t}) \\
  &= \mathcal{F}\left[\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{}x}s(x,t)\right]\cdot\mathcal{F}\left[h(t)\right]\;,
\end{align}
which demonstrates that differentiation is time-invariant, and allows
us to deduce the convolution rule of differentiation --- to
differentiate a convolution we need only differentiate one of the
functions being convolved. Applied to dual numbers,
#+NAME: eq:
\begin{equation}
\langle{}s,\nabla{}s\rangle\ast\langle{}h,\nabla{}h\rangle = \langle{}s\ast{}h, \nabla{}s\ast{}h\rangle\;,
\end{equation}
giving us the following semantic scheme:
#+NAME: eq:diffmem
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \Delta_{N}\llbracket\texttt{mem}\rrbracket &\;:\; \mathbb{S}_{d} \to \mathbb{S}_{d} \\
    \Delta_{N}\llbracket\texttt{mem}\rrbracket(\langle{}s, \nabla{}s\rangle) &\to (\langle{}y, \nabla{}y\rangle) \\
    \langle{}y(t),\nabla{}y(t)\rangle &= \langle{}s(t-1),\nabla{}s(t-1)\rangle\;.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}

**** Recursive Composition

The differentiability of recursive filters is
well-established\nbsp[cite:@shynkAdaptive1989] --- in short, the
derivative of a recursive expression is itself recursive. In the
specific case of Faust's recursive composition operator (see
cref:fig:rec), to establish a rule for derivative transformation we
can take a similar approach to cref:eq:diffSeqSemantic, making \(g\)
an inner function that acts upon a vector of past output signals:
#+NAME: eq:dualrec
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&\langle{}\mathbf{y}(t),\,\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(t)\rangle = \bigg\langle{}f(\mathbf{s}(t),g(\mathbf{y}(t-1))), \\
  &\frac{\partial{}f}{\partial{}\mathbf{s}(t)}\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{s}}(t) +
  \frac{\partial{}f}{\partial{}g(\mathbf{y}(t-1))}\mathbf{J}_{g}(t-1)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(t-1)\bigg\rangle\;.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
In the absence of a feedforward component (notwithstanding the fact
that such a system would produce constant output signals of value
zero), the result simplifies to
#+NAME: eq:all-pole
\begin{equation}
\langle{}f(g(\mathbf{y}(t-1))),\; \mathbf{J}_{f}(t-1)\mathbf{J}_{g}(t-1)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(t-1)\rangle\;,
\end{equation}
which, comparing with cref:eq:diffSeqSemantic, tells us that recursive
composition is simply /sequential composition through time/, and
points to the implicit, and trivially-differentiable, one-sample delay
introduced by Faust's recursive composition operator. We may conclude
that
#+NAME: eq:diffrec
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket A \sim B \rrbracket = \Delta_{N}\llbracket A \rrbracket \sim \Delta_{N}\llbracket B \rrbracket\;.
\end{equation}

**** Merge Composition

Merge composition (cref:fig:merge) entails
summation\nbsp[cite:@orlareySyntactical2004], thus the addition rule of
differentiation must be obeyed when merging dual signals. A merge \(A
:> B\), can be viewed as an interleaving of the outputs of \(A\) into
\(i_{B}\) groups of \(k=o_{A}/i_{B}\) signals, followed by a merging of each
group into one signal path:
#+NAME: eq:expandedMerge
\begin{equation}
A :> B = A \,:\, \chi(i_{B},k) \,:\, \big\|_{l=1}^{i_{B}}\left(
  \big\|_{m=1}^{k}\,\_ \;:>\; \_
\right) : B\;.
\end{equation}
Clearly \(\Delta_{N}\llbracket A:>B \rrbracket\) cannot be defined
recursively, however. Note that for \(i_{B}=1\) the right hand side of
cref:eq:expandedMerge reduces to \(A:>\_\), which is essentially the
term in parenthesis, and can be handled thanks to the definitions in
cref:eq:diffidentity2,eq:diffpar,
#+NAME: eq:mergeOntoOne
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket \big\|_{m=1}^{k}\,\_ \;:>\; \_ \rrbracket
  = \big\|_{m=1}^{k}\Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket \;:>\; \Delta_{N}\llbracket \_ \rrbracket\;.
\end{equation}
The order of signals is maintained, so the resulting dual signal is
guaranteed to be of the desired form
\(\left\langle{}\sum_{m=1}^{k}s_{m},\sum_{l=m}^{k}\nabla{}s_{m}\right\rangle\).  For the full
expression for differentiable merge composition, see
cref:eq:diffmerge.

#+attr_latex: :options float=t, numbers=left, xleftmargin=12pt, framexleftmargin=12pt
#+name: lst:neuron
#+caption: Excerpt from a differentiable artificial neuron
#+caption: implementation. See cref:sec:discussion for
#+caption: detail.
#+begin_src faust :noeval
// diff.lib
neuron(NINPUTS, activationFunction) =
  WX + b : activationFunction
with {
  WX = linalg.dot(NINPUTS);
  b = _;
};

activations = environment {
  sigmoid = *(-1) : 1 / (1 + exp);
}

linalg = environment {
  dot(N) = interleave(N, 2)
    : par(i, N, *) :> _;
};

// neuron.dsp
NW = 3; // Number of weights.

// Send inputs, and weights/biases,
// to the differentiated neuron.
process = par(i, NW, _),
  par(i, NW + 1, wb.var(i + 1))
  : d.diffInN(neuron, NW)
with {
  wb = weightsAndBiases((1, NW));
  d = df.env(wb);
  neuron = d.neuron(NW,
    d.activations.sigmoid);
};
#+end_src

The rules for differentiating Faust's composition operators are
collected in cref:fig:diffRules. With the implementation of these
rules via pattern matching, Faust programs can be recursively
decomposed according to their composition operators, and re-composed
in differentiable fashion, satisfying the syntactic rule in
cref:eq:idealDiffSyntax. For example:
#+NAME: eq:diffComposition
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Delta_{N}\llbracket (A,C):B \rrbracket
  &= \Delta_{N}\llbracket A,C \rrbracket \;:\; \Delta_{N}\llbracket B \rrbracket \\
  &= \Delta_{N}\llbracket A \rrbracket , \Delta_{N}\llbracket C \rrbracket \;:\; \Delta_{N}\llbracket B \rrbracket\;.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
An artificial neuron of the form \(y = \sigma(\mathbf{wx}+b)\), such as
could be used in a binary classification problem, can be created as
per cref:lst:neuron. The code describes a neuron and sigmoid
activation function in primal form, in a library file, =diff.lib=; the
~dot~ function (line 14) makes use of the familiar ~interleave~ function
to compute the dot product, \(\mathbf{wx}\); in =neuron.dsp=,
differentiable weights and biases, ~wb~, are created according to a network
layer specification (line 27) --- one pair of signals indicating
one layer containing one neuron with ~NW~ weights; weights and biases
are used to create a differentiable environment (~d~, line 28; described
in cref:sec:diffprog,lst:diffAdd); the wrapper-function ~diffInN~
(line 25) enacts the recursive derivative transformation; its first ~NW~
inputs are considered input signals, \(\mathbf{s}\), the rest as input
variables, \(\mathbf{x}\).

Worthy of reflection is that algorithms of this sort, consisting of
differentiable primitives, plus our now-differentiable composition
operators, can be automatically differentiated to arbitrary order,
\(\Delta_{N}\llbracket{\scriptstyle\Delta_{N}\llbracket
\cdots}{\scriptscriptstyle\Delta_{N}\llbracket \cdot \rrbracket}{\scriptstyle \cdots
\rrbracket}\rrbracket\).

** Limitations
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:limitations
:END:

Although the scheme presented here renders a great deal of existing
Faust code differentiable, certain primitives and language constructs
cannot, at present, be subjected to AD.

Faust's ~route~ primitive cannot be pattern-matched, which precludes the
derivative transformation of arbitrary signal routing expressions
based on that primitive, such as those in the =routes.lib=
library.[fn:4] As described in cref:sec:cut, however, such expressions
can be recreated with the identity and cut primitives; common routing
tasks like interleaving can be achieved differentiably with code such
as in cref:lst:interleave.

User interface primitives (sliders and buttons, for example) are
matchable in principle, but in practice, ~hslider("x1", 0, -1, 1,
.01)~, whose parameters are a string identifier, initial value,
minimum, maximum and step, must be matched verbatim; clearly, in the
general case, this is not practical. The obvious derivative
transformation of a user interface element is to an input variable,
i.e. a parameter for optimisation such as a weight in a neural
network. Input variables must be identifiable so as to create the
appropriate corresponding dual signal,
\(\big\langle{}x_{i},\begin{bmatrix}0 & \cdots & \frac{\partial{}x_{i}}{\partial{}x_{i}} & \cdots &
0\end{bmatrix}^{T}\big\rangle\). The approach proposed is to move input
variables out of the general AD transformation; a wrapper function is
used to indicate the number of input signals (whose gradients are zero
vectors) and input variables received by the main body of the
differentiated block diagram (see cref:lst:neuron,lst:iir). Though
imposing a certain coding convention, we consider this comparable with
the particular requirements imposed by PyTorch, TensorFlow, and JAX,
with their respective dedicated datatypes for learnable parameters.

Though not a frequently-employed feature of the language, unapplied
lambda abstractions appear in some key definitions, the ~iir~ function
in =filters.lib=, for example. Implemented in
transposed-direct-form\nbsp[cite:@smithIntroduction2008], ~iir~ is defined
with the abstraction ~ma.sub~, which accepts two arguments, ~x~ and ~y~, and
returns the result ~y-x~. Attempting \(\Delta_{N}\llbracket \texttt{sub}
\rrbracket\), the abstraction is essentially treated as a numerical
constant, and given a vector of zeros as its gradient. Fortunately,
constructions of this kind --- in this case reversing signal order
before applying some primitive operation --- are effectively matters
of signal-routing and, as described in cref:sec:cut, can be recreated
using differentiable primitives.

** Applications & Outlook

#+attr_latex: :options float=t, numbers=left, xleftmargin=12pt, framexleftmargin=12pt
#+name: lst:iir
#+caption: Excerpt from a differentiable IIR filter implementation.
#+begin_src faust :noeval
N = 2; // filter order.

// ...Set up AD vars & environment...

// Differentiable TDF IIR filter.
iir = ((sub,par(l, N, _))
  ~ (fir(N))),par(l, N + 1, _)
  : _,par(l, N, !),par(l, N + 1, _)
  : fir(N + 1)
with { 
  sub = _,_ <: !,_,_,! : -;
  fir(n) =
    (_ <: par(l, n, seq(m, l, mem))),
      par(l, n, _) 
    : linalg.dot(n);
};

// Send a signal input, and coeffs,
// to the differentiated filter.
process = _,coeffs : d.diffInN(iir, 1)
with {
    coeffs = par(i, 2 * N + 1,
                 vars.var(i + 1));
};
#+end_src

Despite the above limitations, and beyond the example given in
cref:lst:neuron, the AD system described here can be put to practical
use. A differentiable transposed-direct-form IIR filter implementation
is given in cref:lst:iir; though reliant on some careful signal
routing, particularly for directing coefficients to the feedback
(~fir(N)~, line 7) and feedforward (~fir(N + 1)~, line 9) portions of
the filter, it overcomes the obstacles to differentiability posed by
its counterpart in =filters.lib=. Structurally, it is similar to the
neuron implementation in cref:lst:neuron; parameters, in this case
learnable filter coefficients, are defined outside of the
differentiable environment, and passed, along with an input signal, to
the wrapper function ~diffInN~. An expanded version of this example
(plus that given in cref:lst:neuron) is available online[fn:5] and can
be run in Faust's web IDE.[fn:6] Comparing, via a differentiable loss
function, the output of the filter against that of an equivalent
filter with fixed, /hidden/ parameters, and backpropagating the
resulting gradients, learnable parameters can be optimised in real
time. This generalises to filters of higher order and those described
by metaparameters such as cutoff frequency, and the length of multiple
hidden delay lines can be found by optimisation against a
differentiable FIR filterbank; these examples point the way to a
variety of real-time adaptive processing applications.

Though demonstrated with /known/ algorithms, in principle the above can
be extended to optimisation against a target /black box/ filter, granted
that both target and estimate receive the same input signal. This
hints, however, at another limitation of the current approach ---
Faust is primarily concerned with time-domain operations, thus loss is
computed in the time domain on a per-sample basis (albeit with the
possibility of taking a running-average of recent sample values, for
example). For phase-invariant and perceptually-informed optimisation
scenarios, frequency-domain loss computation is required; the recent
introduction of multi-rate processing to the Faust language promises
to enable spectral signal analysis. Developments of this sort will
improve the feasibility of taking on larger-scale, typically
deferred-time problems such neural audio synthesis. These call for
generalisation to a corpus of training data (rather than optimisation
to a small number of input variables); with that end in mind, the
example in cref:lst:neuron should be extended to a general neural
network structure, and a supporting infrastructure created for
delivering training data and capturing weights and biases for
real-time inference.

** Conclusion

In this chapter a general scheme for differentiating Faust programs
has been described, moving an important step toward providing the
means with which to tackle audio optimisation and machine learning
problems with the domain-specific and cross-platform benefits of the
Faust language. A variety of time-domain, gradient-based optimisation
problems can be tackled in Faust, with results produced in real-time.

Many challenges and opportunities remain as impetus for further
development of the work presented here. Having only been put to
informal use to-date, the proposed AD approach should be assessed for
its efficacy and efficiency. Beyond the correctness of the semantic
scheme, being based on forward-mode AD, which, on paper, compares
unfavourably with reverse-mode in terms of computational expense, its
combination with the optimisations of the Faust compiler should be
explored. At the level of the Faust compiler, changes that would
facilitate the transformation of elements of the language that
currently elude pattern matching would be of benefit here, as would
further development of Faust's adaptability to frequency domain
processing.

Time is an illusion; lunchtime doubly so.

\lettrine{C}{onsider} a tuning fork, a steel, two-pronged resonator
which, when struck, vibrates at a reliable frequency. Just how
reliable is this frequency when all is said and done? Frequency, given
in \unit{\hertz}, can be said to describe the number of times a given
event occurs in one second. And how is a second defined? In terms of
the duration required for a caesium atom to vibrate a certain number
of times\nbsp[cite:@markowitzDefinition1961]. So, frequency is defined in
terms of time, and time in terms of frequency; it appears that we have
a tautology on our hands. Indeed, this is an inescapable reality by
which we are faced when we wrestle intimately with time, that most
illusory of concepts.

Returning to our tuning fork, each time it is struck, the steel from
which it is manufactured will deform ever so slightly. Some of the
physical energy imparted to it by the blow against a hard surface will
be transduced to heat, which changes the physical behaviour of its
material. Strike it on a cold day and it will vibrate more quickly
than on a hot day. And that is not even to mention the device against
which it was calibrated on the day it was manufactured. In short, a
tuning fork designed to produce a reliable \qty{440}{\hertz} frequency
/does not/, /has never/, and /will never/ produce precisely that
frequency. Indeed, given that atomic clocks are /only/ accurate to
parts per trillion [cite:@gillWhen2011;
@markowitzDefinition1961],[fn:1] the very idea of the quantity
\qty{440}{\hertz} is far from perfectly-defined.

Time in computer systems is, of course, subject to the same
vagaries. It is in fact something like a tuning fork that describes
the idea of time in the vast majority of digital systems. A crystal
oscillator (XO), is a sliver of piezoelectric quartz mineral or
ceramic, and, like a tuning fork, on receiving some energy (in this
case electrical) as input, vibrates at a more-or-less reliable
frequency. How accurate this frequency is (setting aside the
fallibility of the definition of a second) is typically defined in
/parts-per-million/ (ppm); a XO with a tolerance of 10 ppm can
reasonably be expected to vibrate up to 0.001 % faster or slower than
the frequency to which it is rated. Thus a 10 ppm XO manufactured to
vibrate at \qty{1}{\mega\hertz} my be expected to produce a frequency,
\(f\), in the range

#+NAME: eq:freq-range
\begin{equation}
999\,990 \leq f \leq 1\,000\,010\;.
\end{equation}
If, to the (audio) engineer's eye, those figures suddenly don't look
so reliable after all, that is entirely understandable. What of the
purity, the /truth/ of the audio sampling rate? Indeed, an audio
system governed by a 10 ppm XO, running at a nominal sampling
frequency of \qty{48}{\kilo\hertz} may run as slow as \(f_{s_{1}} = \)
\qty{47999.52}{\hertz} or as fast as \(f_{s_{2}} = \)
\qty{48000.48}{\hertz}. Is this sort of difference perceptible? By way
of context, that difference is analogous to having two A_{4} tuning
forks, one producing a frequency of \qty{439.9956}{\hertz} and another
at \qty{440.0044}{\hertz}, so a difference of less than one hundredth
of a cycle per second (one twentieth of a cent); this falls far, far
below the /just noticeable difference/ threshold for human pitch
perception\nbsp[cite:@zwickerPsychoacoustics2010]. The audio engineer
notes, however, that the devices running at \(f_{s_{1}}\) and \(f_{s_{2}}\)
will, after one second, be out of sync by almost the duration of one
full audio sample (\sim\qty{21}{\micro\second} at \qty{48}{\kilo\hertz}); the
DSP engineer notes that the sum two audio signals, \(x_{1}[n]\) and
\(x_{2}[n]\), where \(x_{2}[n] = x_{1}[n-1]\) is a one-zero low-pass
filter\nbsp[cite:@mcclellanSignal2003].

** Network Protocols for Time Exchange

*** Network Time Protocol

*** Precision Time Protocol

#+name: fig:ptp
#+caption: PTP packet flow.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}
  \pic{ptp};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{figure}

IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) is a time exchange mechanism
consisting of the exchange of multicast network packets between a
clock authority and a clock subscriber\nbsp[cite:@leeProceedings2005;
@schleusnerSubMicrosecond2024]. PTP packet flow is illustrated in
cref:fig:ptp. Flow begins with the clock authority sending a /sync/
packet; the subscriber notes the time, \(t_{2}\), at which this packet
arrives. The authority then sends a /follow-up/ packet which is
timestamped, \(t_{1}\), with the time of transmission. With \(t_{1}\)
and \(t_{2}\) in hand, the subscriber sends a /delay request/ packet
and notes the time, \(t_{3}\) of transmission; the authority notes the
time, \(t_{4}\) of reception of this packet and sends it to the
subscriber in a final /delay response/ packet. The above process then
repeats for the next time period, typically a second, beginning with
the receipt time \(t'_{2}\)of the following sync packet and the
receipt of the next follow-up packet containing timestamp
\(t'_{1}\). Once the subscriber has the above timestamp information it
can begin to compute its drift, \(\alpha\), delay, \(\delta\), and
offset, \(\theta\) relative to the authority:

#+NAME: eq:ptp1
\begin{align}
\alpha &= 1 - \frac{t'_{2} - t_{2}}{t'_{1} - t_{1}}\;, \\
\delta &= \frac{(t_{4} - t_{1}) - (t_{3} - t_{2})}{2}\;, \\
\theta &= t_{2} - t_{1} - \delta\;.
\end{align}

Armed with the above figures, the subscriber can feed them into a
proportional integral (PI) controller to yield a number of nanoseconds
for second \(n\) by which it should adjust its internal clock,

#+NAME: eq:ptp-pi
\begin{equation}
a_{n} = \alpha - K_{P}\theta_{n} + K_{I}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\theta\;,
\end{equation}
where \(K_{P}\) and \(K_{I}\) are co-efficients to be applied to the
proportional and integral terms respectively; small co-efficients,
typically \(K_{I} < K_{P} < 1\). The adjustment value \(a_{n}\) can be used to
set, for example, the per-cycle increment of a hardware ethernet timer
register.

** Media Clock Recovery

Time exchange at the level of the ethernet subsystems of distributed
modules does not directly help us achieve audio sampling rate
parity. The time exchange information derived from PTP must be used to
condition the audio clocks of the modules such that sampling rate
parity is achieved; this is a process referred to as /media clock
recovery/\nbsp[cite:@crankshawMedia2021].

In the case of the iMXRT1062 MCU this process is achieved by using the
nanoseconds-per-second PTP-derived adjustment value at second \(n\),
\(a_{n}\), to adjust the sampling rate. For a nominal sampling rate
\(f_{s}\) (e.g. \qty{48}{\kHz}), the sampling rate, \(\hat{f}_{s,n}\) that
"matches" that of the time authority is found simply as

#+NAME: eq:sampling-rate-recovery-1
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}_{s,n} = f_{s}(1 + a_{n})\;.
\end{equation}
For example, if \(a_{n} = -1750\;\text{ns}\), we have

#+begin_src emacs-lisp :exports none
(let* ((fs 48000)
       (a -1750e-9)
       (proportion (+ 1 a))
       (fhats (* fs proportion)))
  (format "%.9f %f" proportion fhats))
#+end_src

#+RESULTS:
: 0.999998250 47999.916000

#+NAME: eq:adjust-example
\begin{align*}
\hat{f}_{s,n} &= f_{s}(1 - 1750 \times 10^{-9}) \\
  &= 48\,000 \times 0.999\,998\,25 \\
  &= 47\,999.916\;\text{Hz}\;.
\end{align*}
Inserting this value into cref:eq:general-fs, the expression for the
audio sampling rate of the iMXRT1062, yields

#+NAME: eq:adjusted-fs
\begin{equation}
47\,999.916 = \frac{24 \times 10^{6}}{2^{8}}\frac{D_{S} + \frac{D_{N,n}}{D_{D}}}{D_{p}D_{A}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}}\;,
\end{equation}
and rearranging, using cref:eq:unknown-dn-2 to find the appropriate
value for the PLL4 numerator register

#+NAME: eq:adjusted-dn
\begin{equation}
D_{N,n} = \frac{D_{D}}{1 \times 10^{6}}\left(
  \frac{2^{8} \times 47\,999.916 \times D_{p}D_{A}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}}{24} -
  1 \times 10^{6} D_{S}
\right)\;.
\end{equation}
Using the values derived in cref:sec:setting-the-sampling-rate yields

#+NAME: eq:adjusted-dn-2
\begin{align*}
D_{N,n} &= 1 \times 10^{3}\left(
  \frac{2^{8} \times 47\,999.916 \times 3 \times 19}{24} -
  29 \times 10^{6}
\right) \\
  &= 183\,948\,928\;,
\end{align*}
which can be used as a new value for the ~CCM_ANALOG_PLL_AUDIO_NUM~
register of the iMXRT1062 MCU.

In practice cref:eq:sampling-rate-recovery-1 does not suffice,
however, and some residual parts per billion drift remains (see
cref:fig:drift). The proposed solution is as follows: first, observing
that at a nominal sampling rate of \qty{48}{\kHz} for power of two
audio buffer sizes less than or equal to 128 there will be an integer
number, \(I\), of audio interrupts per second, take a representative
nanoseconds figure, \(t_{S}\), on audio system startup; second, every
\(I\) interrupts, reading the system's PTP timer, taking the
nanoseconds part, \(t_{\text{PTP}}\) and comparing with \(t_{S}\):

#+NAME: eq:audio-ptp-offset
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{n} = t_{\text{PTP}} - t_{S}\;;
\end{equation}
finally, applying another PI controller:

#+NAME: eq:sampling-rate-recovery-2
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}_{s,n} = f_{s}\left(
  1 + a_{n} + K_{P_{\Delta}}\Delta_{n} + K_{I_{\Delta}}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\Delta
\right)\;.
\end{equation}
These additional correction factors ensure reliable media clock
recovery indefinitely.

** Presentation Time Recovery

If media clock recovery is a question of parity of rate of
reproduction then presentation time recovery is one of simultaneity of
reproduction. If media clocks are synchronised but audio interrupts
are not, clients will reproduce audio at the same rate but with some
constant temporal offset. Once again, the solution to this problem
lies in judicious selection of sampling rate, \(f_{s}\), and audio buffer
size, \(N\), plus co-ordination via PTP.

Due to the way that DMA is configured, the iMXRT1062 generates two
interrupts per audio buffer. At a sampling rate of
\qty{48}{\kilo\hertz} and buffer size of 256 or below this means that
an integer number of interrupts are generated each second. On system
startup, waiting for a PTP lock to be achieved, it is possible to
ensure that the audio subsystem starts up on a given PTP second and
thus that audio subsystems on the entire network are synchronised.

Before proceeding to a discussion of /time exchange/ mechanisms, it is
important to deal with how audio timing is configured on our target
hardware platform, the Teensy 4.1 and its NXP iMXRT1062 MCU.

The iMXRT1062 features a dedicated audio phase locked loop (PLL),
PLL4, and a /synchronous audio interface/, SAI1, the latter providing
I^{2}S output. By convention, the output frequency produced by SAI1,
~SAI1_CLK_ROOT~, \(f_{R}\), must be 256 times oversampled with respect
to the target audio sampling rate, \(f_{s}\):

#+NAME: eq:sai1-1
\begin{equation}
f_{R} = 2^{8}f_{s}\;.
\end{equation}

The NXP MCUXpresso Config Tools software\nbsp[cite:@MCUXpresso2018]
indicates that \(f_{R}\) must be lower than or equal to
\qty{66}{\MHz}, therefore

#+begin_src emacs-lisp :exports none

#+NAME: eq:max-fs
\begin{equation}
f_{s} \leq 257\,812.5\;\text{Hz}\;.
\end{equation}
\(f_{R}\) is found by dividing the frequency of ~PLL4_MAIN_CLK~,
\(f_{M}\), by the ~SAI1_CLK_PRED~, \(D_{I_{1}}\), and ~SAI1_CLK_PODF~,
\(D_{I_{2}}\) fields of the ~CCM_CS1CDR~ register:

#+NAME: eq:sai1-2
\begin{equation}
f_{R} = \frac{f_{M}}{D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}}\;.
\end{equation}
\(f_{M}\) is found by dividing \(f_{P}\), the frequency of PLL4, by
the ~POST_DIV_SELECT~, \(D_{p}\), and ~AUDIO _DIV~, \(D_{A}\), registers:

#+NAME: eq:pll4-main
\begin{equation}
f_{M} = \frac{f_{P}}{D_{p}D_{A}}\;.
\end{equation}
\(f_{P}\) is the result of multiplying the iMXRT1062's \qty{24}{\MHz}
clock source, \(f_{XO}\), by the PLL4 divider, \(D_{P}\):

#+NAME: eq:pll4
\begin{equation}
f_{P} = f_{XO}D_{P}\;,
\end{equation}
and \(D_{P}\) is, in turn, the sum of the /PLL loop divider/, the
~DIV_SELECT~ field of the ~CCM_ANALOG_PLL~ ~_AUDIO~ register, \(D_{S}\), with
the quotient of ~CCM_ANALOG_PLL_AUDIO_NUM~, \(D_{N}\), the /PLL
fractional loop divider/ numerator register and
~CCM_ANALOG_PLL_AUDIO_DENOM~, \(D_{D}\), the denominator register:

#+NAME: eq:pll4-divider
\begin{equation}
D_{P} = D_{S} + \frac{D_{N}}{D_{D}}\;.
\end{equation}
See cref:fig:imxrt1062-clock for a diagrammatic representation of the
effect of the audio PLL and clock divider registers on the
\qty{24}{\MHz} clock source.

The ~DIV_SELECT~ field has a valid range of \(27 \leq D_{S} \leq 54\),
and \(|D_{N}| < D_{D}\), so \(D_{P}\) describes (in theory) a number in the
following range:

#+NAME: eq:divider-range
\begin{equation}
\left(26 + \frac{1}{D_{D}}\right) \leq D_{P} \leq \left(54 + \frac{D_{D}-1}{D_{D}}\right)\;,
\end{equation}
with \(1 \leq D_{D} \leq 2^{30}\); effectively, \(26 < D_{P} <
55\). MCUXpresso Config Tools, and the iMXRT1062 reference

**** A General Expression for the Sampling Rate

Bringing all of the above together, the general expression for
achieving a target sampling rate \(\hat{f}_{s}\) is

#+NAME: eq:general-fs
\begin{align}
\hat{f}_{s} &= \frac{f_{R}}{2^{8}} \nonumber \\
  &= \frac{f_{M}}{2^{8}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}} \nonumber \\
  &= \frac{f_{XO}D_{P}}{2^{8}D_{p}D_{A}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}} \nonumber \\
  &= \frac{24\times10^{6}}{2^{8}}\frac{D_{S}+\frac{D_{N}}{D_{D}}}{D_{p}D_{A}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}}\;.
\end{align}
This reckoning alone does not take into account certain constraints on
the system, however, such as cref:eq:pll4-range and the frequency at
the input of \(D_{I_{2}}\) being less than \qty{300}{\MHz} (see
cref:fig:imxrt1062-clock). There are too many free variables to solve
for any one of them, so an iterative approach to finding appropriate
register and field values is necessary.

**** Setting the Sampling Rate
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sec:setting-the-sampling-rate
:END:

#+name: alg:sampling-rate
#+caption: Algorithm for finding ideal divider register values for a
#+caption: given sampling rate.
\begin{algorithm}
  \tcc{Order of magnitude by which to scale calculations.}
  \(O_{m}= 1 \times 10^{6}\)

  \For{\(D_{S} \leftarrow 27\) \KwTo \(54\)}{
    \(D_{N} = 0\)

    \(D_{D} = O_{m}\)

    \(D_{I_{1}} = 1\)

    \(D_{I_{2}} = 1\)

    \(d_{e} = -1\) \tcc{Initial exact divider}
    \While{\(f_{I_{2}} \leq 300\;\text{MHz}\) and \(D_{I_{1}} < D_{I_{1},MAX}\)}{
      \(+\!+D_{I_{1}}\)
    }
    \While{\(d_{e}/O_{m} \neq D_{S}\)}{
      \(+\!+D_{I_{2}}\)

      \If{\(\hat{f}_{s} > f_{s,MAX}\) and \(D_{I_{1}} < D_{I_{1},MAX}\)}{
        \(+\!+D_{I_{1}}\)

        \(D_{I_{2}} = 1\)

        continue
      }
      \(d_{e} = 2^{8}\hat{f}_{s}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}/24\)

      \If{\(\lfloor{}d_{e}/O_{m}\rfloor \neq D_{S}\)}{
        \If{\(D_{I_{1}} < D_{I_{1},MAX}\)}{
          \(+\!+D_{I_{1}}\)

          \(D_{I_{2}} = 1\)

          continue
        } \Else {break}
      }

      \(D_{N} = d_{e} - O_{m}D_{S}\)

      \While{\(D_{N} \times 10 < D_{N,MAX}\) and \(D_{D} \times 10 < D_{D,MAX}\)}{
        \(D_{N}\;\ast\!= 10\)

        \(D_{D}\;\ast\!= 10\)
      }

      \If{\(f_{P}\) is valid and \(D_{D} = 1 \times 10^{9}\)}{
        \Return
      } \Else {break}
    }
  }
\end{algorithm}

Say we wish to set a sampling rate of \(f_{s} = 48\;\text{kHz}\); our
target \(f_{R}\), \(\hat{f}_{R}\) equals, as per cref:eq:sai1-1

#+NAME: eq:sai1-clk-root
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}_{R} = 48\,000 \times 2^{8} = 12\,288\,000\;\text{Hz}\;.
\end{equation}
Strategically leaving \(D_{p}\) and \(D_{A}\) set to their default
values of 1, cref:alg:sampling-rate can be used to find values for
\(D_{S}\), \(D_{N}\), \(D_{D}\), \(D_{I_{1}}\) and \(D_{I_{2}}\).

#+begin_src julia :results output :eval never-export :exports results
using Printf, Format

let
    # This will simplify some of the calculations, and should help
    # with getting accurate results, particularly in C++.
    MHz = 1e6
    oscMHz = 24;
    oscHz = oscMHz * MHz
    oversamplingRatio = 1 << 8
    Fs = 48000
    fR = Fs * oversamplingRatio
    # Adjustment to all figures so we can compare integer targets...
    cyclesPerOversample = oscHz/oversamplingRatio
    # Frequency limitaitions
    pll4Min = 650 * MHz
    pll4Max = 1300 * MHz
    sai1PostMaxInputFreq = 300 * MHz
    numMax = (1 << 29) - 1
    denomMax = (1 << 30) - 1

    @printf "Target sample rate:   %s Hz\n" cfmt("%'d", Fs)
    @printf "Target SAI1_CLK_ROOT: %s Hz\n\n" cfmt("%'d", fR)

    @printf "fXO * (DS + DN / DD)/(DA * Dp * DI1 * DI2) = %f Hz\n" fR

    for div in 27:54
        num = 0            # Initial numerator
        denom = 1e6        # Start with millionths
        sai1Pre = 1        # Initial SAI1 pre-divider
        sai1Post = 1       # and post-divider
        divExact = -1.0        

        pllFreq = oscHz * (div + num / denom)

        # Adjust first sai1 divider to get a valid input to the second
        # divider
        while pllFreq / sai1Pre > sai1PostMaxInputFreq && sai1Pre < 8
            sai1Pre += 1
        end

        while floor(divExact / MHz) != div
            # Adjust second sai1 divider to get a value below the target
            while cyclesPerOversample * div / (sai1Pre * sai1Post) > Fs && sai1Post < 64
                sai1Post += 1
            end

            # If target Fs is greater than the current achievable Fs,
            # try another combination of SAI1 dividers.
            if (Fs > ((div + 1)/(sai1Pre * sai1Post)) * cyclesPerOversample
                && sai1Pre < 8)
                sai1Pre += 1
                sai1Post = 1
                continue
            end

            # Divide by the main oscillator's frequency in MHz to get
            # a better-behaved number with which to calculate.
            divExact = Fs * oversamplingRatio * sai1Pre * sai1Post / oscMHz

            # If fractional and integer div don't have the same
            # integer part, something's wrong; try a different SAI1
            # predivider.
            if floor(divExact / MHz) != div
                if sai1Pre < 8
                    sai1Pre += 1
                    sai1Post = 1
                    continue
                else
                    break
                end
            end

            num = floor(divExact - div * MHz)

            # Scale num and denom to take greatest possible advantage
            # of the available numerical resolution.
            while num * 10 < numMax && denom * 10 < denomMax
                num *= 10;
                denom *= 10;
            end

            pll4Freq = oscHz * (div + num/denom)

Say a set of divider register values has been established using
cref:alg:sampling-rate, and some manner of fine adjustment is desired,
e.g. to compensate for clock drift. Cref:eq:general-fs can be
rewritten to find an expression for an unknown \(D_{N}\):

\begin{equation}
D_{N} = D_{D}\left(
  \frac{2^{8}\hat{f}_{s}D_{p}D_{A}D_{I_{1}}D_{I_{2}}}{24 \times 10^{6}} - D_{S}

* Protocol and Software Systems

The heart of the system is /Ananas/, a networked audio system for
time-sensitive applications. Ananas, the French word for "pineapple",
is a recursive acronym: /"Ananas necessitates another networked audio
system"/. It consists of a networked audio server, protocols for audio
and control data transmission and device discovery, media clock and
presentation time recovery, plus microcontroller firmware and software
plugins for sound field synthesis control.

** The Audio Server

The Ananas audio server is a C++ library, written using the JUCE
framework, containing a class, ~ananas::Server::Server~, that inherits
from ~juce::AudioSource~ and encapsulates a collection (a
~juce::OwnedArray~) of threads. Each thread is responsible for a given
server task, be it sending audio data to the network, listening for
PTP timestamps, listening for client and time authority announcement
packets, sending client reboot instructions, or inspecting connected
switches. The audio transmission part of the server is illustrated in
cref:fig:server. The server receives a collection of input channels,
either monophonic WFS sources or ACN encoded audio channels, and
writes these to an intermediate first-in-first-out (FIFO) buffer. When
the FIFO contains enough samples to fill an outgoing audio packet, the
sender thread is notified and reads from the FIFO to fill a packet for
transmission. The sender thread delivers the packet to a UDP multicast
socket and transmits it to the network.

** The Ananas Protocol
*** The Ananas Audio Protocol

Being first and foremost concerned with the transmission of audio
data, at the heart of the Ananas system is the Ananas Audio Protocol
(AAP). A UDP multicast-based protocol, using address =224.4.224.4=,
taken from the second ad-hoc block of the Internet Assigned Numbers
Authority's multicast address space\nbsp[cite:@meyerIANA2010], AAP
consists of a stream of sequence-numbered, timestamped packets. The
structure of an AAP packet is given in cref:fig:audio-packet. It was
found that, particularly under high-bandwidth conditions, packets
sporadically arrive out of order, hence the inclusion of the sequence
number, which is used at the receiving side to ensure the integrity of
the packet stream. For timestamping, the server reads a PTP follow-up
packet, adds a small reproduction offset (for packets of 16 frames it
was found that an offset of \(1/70\) s was most appropriate) and
writes the timestamp to the outgoing packet; successive packets are
then updated with the expected inter-packet interval, e.g., for 16
frames at \qty{48}{\kilo\hertz} this is \(333\,333\frac{1}{3}\) ns.

Completing the packet header are the number of channels and number of
frames in the packet, used at the receving side for decoding the
byte-encoded audio data that follows. At 16 frames per packet it is
possible to transmit up to 44 channels of 16-bit audio while
respecting ethernet's 1500 byte maximum transmissible unit.

*** The Ananas Authority Description Protocol

The time authority announces its presence on the network with UDP
multicast packets on address =224.4.224.6= port 49173. It sends packets
containing its hardware-derived serial number plus the value of its
current USB audio feedback accumulator for consumption by the server;
the server is aware of the default value of the feedback accumulator
and can thus calculate the USB audio sampling rate that the time
authority is currently requesting. This is useful primarily for
debugging purposes; the expectation is that as client audio buffers
fill up the feedback accumulator value will fall and vice versa. The
structure of an Ananas Authority Description Protocol packet is given
in cref:fig:authority-packet.

Clients announce their presence on the network with periodic UDP
multicast packets on address =224.4.224.6= port 49172. A client is
deemed to be connected if the server has received an Ananas Client
Description Protocol (ACDP) packet within the last second. Each packet
contains a variety of information about a given client, such as its
serial number, firmware type and version, reported sampling rate,
buffer fill percentage and PTP lock status, etc., information which
can be displayed in a digital audio workstation plugin interface. The
structure of an ACDP packet is given in cref:fig:client-packet.
